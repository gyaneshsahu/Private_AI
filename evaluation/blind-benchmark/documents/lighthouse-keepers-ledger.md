# The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

*Synthetic short story written for a blind benchmark. All characters and places are fictional.*

Maren Holt had kept the light on Skerra Point for nineteen years, and in all that time the ledger had never lied to her.

It lived on the shelf above the oil store, a long book bound in green cloth that had gone grey at the corners. Every delivery went into it: the date, the supply boat, the number of drums, the weight of each drum, the signature of whoever had carried them up the slipway. Every night's burning went into it too, measured off the gauge on the big tank in the lamp room. The two columns, what came in and what went out, were supposed to meet at the end of every month like two ends of a rope. For nineteen years they had.

In the autumn of the year the harbour board changed the tenders, they stopped meeting.

The first month the gap was small enough to be a mistake: eleven litres, which she put down to a sticky gauge. The second month it was forty. The third month, after the October delivery, it was ninety-two litres, nearly half a drum, and Maren sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open and the lamp turned low and felt something cold settle in her stomach.

There were only three people who came and went from Skerra Point. There was Maren herself. There was her nephew Tobias, fifteen years old, sent out from the mainland in September after his mother's illness, who spent his days doing correspondence lessons at the same kitchen table and his evenings on the rocks with a fishing line. And there was Idris Vale, who ran the supply boat *Curlew* out from Harrow Quay every second Thursday with oil, flour, post and whatever else the board decided the light needed.

She did not want it to be Tobias. She told herself that was not a reason to think it wasn't.

---

She watched the next delivery from the lamp gallery, where she could see the slipway without being seen. Idris brought the *Curlew* in neatly on the rising tide, as he always did. He was a broad, quiet man in his sixties with a grey beard and hands like coiled rope, and he had run the island routes for longer than Maren had kept the light. He and Tobias rolled six drums up the slipway on the trolley, one at a time, and stacked them in the oil store. Idris signed the ledger in his square capitals. Six drums, two hundred litres each.

When the *Curlew* had gone, Maren went down with the dipstick and measured each drum. Five were full. The sixth was short by sixty litres, and the seal on its cap had been cut and pressed back so carefully that she had to hold it to the light to see it.

So it was Idris. The thought did not bring her any relief. Idris had pulled her brother out of the water off Harrow Head twenty years ago. Idris had brought medicine across in the January storm when Tobias's grandmother was dying on the island. If Idris Vale was stealing oil from the light, there had to be a reason, and she was afraid of what it would be.

---

She said nothing to Tobias. But Tobias, it turned out, had been watching her watching.

"You measured the drums," he said that evening, not looking up from his algebra. "After the boat."

"I measure everything."

"Not usually after the boat. Usually on Sunday." He put his pencil down. "Is something wrong with the oil?"

She considered lying to him and found she was too tired. She showed him the ledger, the two columns that did not meet, the cut seal.

Tobias looked at the numbers for a long time. Then he said, "Mr Vale's been taking drums off at Corrie."

Corrie was the small island two miles south, with a school of nine children, a chapel and a harbour too shallow for anything bigger than the *Curlew*. The supply boat called there on the way back to Harrow Quay.

"How do you know that?"

"I go fishing off the north rocks. You can see Corrie harbour from there with your binoculars." He had the decency to look embarrassed about the binoculars. "He stops at the school landing, not the harbour. He carries something heavy up. Twice I've seen it."

---

Maren took the dinghy across to Corrie the next Saturday, on the pretext of buying eggs.

The schoolhouse was a single room with a stove at one end and nine desks at the other, and it was warm. That was the first thing she noticed: warm, in November, on an island where the coal boat had not called since the end of August. The teacher, a young woman named Ailsa Brennan who had come out from the mainland the previous year, made her tea on a small paraffin heater at the back of the room, and Maren saw a drum standing against the wall behind the stove with the board's stencil on its side.

She did not have to ask. Ailsa saw her looking and went pale, and then, being honest, told her everything. The board had cut the coal allowance for the Corrie school when the tenders changed, on the grounds that nine children were too few to justify a delivery. Ailsa had written three letters and received one reply, saying that the matter was under review. By October the children were doing their lessons in coats and gloves, and two of the youngest had been ill for weeks. Idris, who had five grandchildren in that school, had started bringing a few litres from each delivery to the light. He had paid for a converter for the stove out of his own pocket.

"He said the light wouldn't miss it," Ailsa said. "He said you kept so much in reserve."

That was true, as far as it went. Maren kept a reserve because of the winter storms, when the *Curlew* could not cross for weeks at a time.

---

The storm came in the second week of December.

It came faster than the forecast said, a south-westerly that built through the afternoon until the spray was coming over the top of the lamp gallery. The *Curlew* was due that Thursday and did not come. By the evening the wind had reached a strength Maren had seen only twice before, and at nine o'clock the main light went out.

It was not the oil. It was the burner, a cracked fitting on the vaporiser that she had been nursing since the summer. She had a spare, but fitting it meant shutting off the supply, draining the line and working by torchlight in a lamp room that was shaking with every gust. It would take forty minutes if nothing went wrong. Somewhere out in the dark, ships would be looking for the light on Skerra Point to find the channel between the Point and the Corrie reef.

"What do I do?" said Tobias, white-faced in the doorway.

"The emergency lamp. On the gallery. You light it and you keep it lit, whatever happens, until I tell you."

The emergency lamp was a big old paraffin lantern, much weaker than the main light but enough to mark the Point. It burned from its own small tank. Tobias lit it with shaking hands on the third try and then stood beside it on the open gallery with a rope tied round his waist, shielding it from the gusts with his body, for an hour and ten minutes, while Maren worked on the burner below him with blood running from a cut on her thumb.

The main light came back at ten past ten. At twenty past, through the rain, Maren saw the lights of a coaster passing safely through the channel, much closer to the reef than she would have liked.

The emergency lamp's tank was nearly empty when Tobias came in. It had held enough paraffin for about ninety minutes.

"If it had been longer," he said, and did not finish.

"If it had been longer," said Maren, "we'd have needed the reserve."

They both knew how much of the reserve had gone to Corrie.

---

Idris came out three days later, when the sea had calmed. He knew as soon as he saw Maren's face. He sat down at the kitchen table, in front of the ledger, and put his big hands flat on the cloth cover.

"I'd do it again," he said. "I'm sorry for the lying. I'm not sorry for the children."

"I know," said Maren. "I'd do it again too, probably. But the light can't be what pays for the board's mistakes, Idris. Not with the reserve. Not in December."

They talked for a long time. Tobias, who was supposed to be doing his algebra, listened to every word.

In the end, it was Tobias who suggested what they did. Maren wrote the missing oil into the ledger, every litre of it, in a new line under the October and November entries: *Lent to Corrie School for heating, by agreement of the keeper, to be repaid.* She signed it, and Idris signed it under her name in his square capitals. Then the three of them wrote a letter to the harbour board, and Ailsa Brennan added a page, and the nine children of Corrie school each signed it too, some of them in crayon. The letter set out what had happened, the cut coal allowance, the cold classroom, the oil, the storm and the forty minutes of darkness on Skerra Point. It asked the board to restore the school's heating allowance and to replace the oil lent from the light's reserve.

Maren did not know whether the board would answer. She did not know whether she would still have a job when it did. But when she put the ledger back on the shelf above the oil store, for the first time in three months, the two columns met.

*The end.*
