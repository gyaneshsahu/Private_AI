## Abstract

We surveyed first- and second-year students at Lindenfeld College about their sleep, study habits, part-time work and use of study techniques during the winter examination period, and linked their answers, with consent, to their average mark in the January examinations. Of 624 students invited, 212 completed the survey (a response rate of 34 per cent). Students who reported sleeping seven hours or more on a typical weeknight had a higher mean examination mark (71.3 out of 100) than students who reported sleeping less than seven hours (66.8). Students who reported using spaced repetition, reviewing material at increasing intervals, had a mean mark of 72.9, compared with 67.0 for those who did not. Students working more than 15 hours a week in paid jobs had lower marks on average. These are associations in a small, self-selected sample, measured mostly by self-report. They do not show that more sleep or a particular study technique causes better results, and we caution readers against interpreting them that way.

## 1. Background

The Learning Support Unit runs workshops on study skills and wellbeing for around 1,200 students each year. Staff and students often ask which habits are most associated with doing well in examinations. The published research literature contains many studies on sleep and learning, on spaced repetition and on the effects of paid work during studies, but we wanted to understand the situation of our own students, who are mostly the first in their family to attend higher education and who often combine study with paid work and caring responsibilities.

The aim of this survey was descriptive: to find out what our students actually do, and to see whether the patterns reported elsewhere are visible in our own data. It was not designed to test any intervention.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Participants

All 624 first- and second-year students enrolled in the four departments of the college (Business, Health Sciences, Engineering Technology and Media) were invited by email to take part. Participation was voluntary and anonymous to the research team: a member of the registry staff not involved in the analysis matched survey responses to examination marks and then removed names and student numbers before the data were passed on.

212 students completed the survey, a response rate of 34 per cent. Respondents were 58 per cent women, 40 per cent men, and 2 per cent described their gender in another way or preferred not to say. Their median age was 21. Health Sciences students were over-represented (39 per cent of respondents, compared with 28 per cent of invited students) and Engineering Technology students were under-represented (14 per cent of respondents, compared with 25 per cent of invited students).

### 2.2 Survey

The online survey had 31 questions and took about twelve minutes to complete. It asked about:

- typical hours of sleep on weeknights and at weekends during the four weeks before the January examinations;
- the number of hours spent studying outside classes per week;
- which study techniques students used, chosen from a list with short descriptions (re-reading notes, highlighting, summarising, practice testing, spaced repetition, group study, and "other");
- hours of paid work per week;
- caring responsibilities;
- use of caffeine and energy drinks;
- a short wellbeing questionnaire.

All of these measures are self-reported. Students were asked to think back over the examination period, so their answers may be affected by memory and by what they think is the "right" answer.

### 2.3 Examination marks

The outcome was each student's average mark across all January examinations, on the college's 0–100 scale. Marks come from college records and are not self-reported.

### 2.4 Analysis

We compared mean marks between groups and calculated simple correlations. Because of the small sample, we did not attempt complex statistical models, and the differences reported below should be read as rough indications. Where we give a range, it is a 95 per cent confidence interval for the difference between group means.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Sleep

On weeknights, 41 per cent of respondents (87 students) reported sleeping seven hours or more, and 59 per cent (125 students) reported sleeping less than seven hours. Only 9 per cent reported sleeping less than five hours.

| Weeknight sleep | Students | Mean exam mark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7 hours or more | 87 | 71.3 |
| Less than 7 hours | 125 | 66.8 |

The difference in means is 4.5 points (95 per cent confidence interval 1.2 to 7.8 points). Students who reported less sleep also reported more hours of paid work and more caffeine use, so the sleep groups differ in other ways too.

Weekend sleep showed no clear relationship with examination marks.

### 3.2 Study techniques

The most commonly reported techniques were re-reading notes (81 per cent) and highlighting (64 per cent). Practice testing was reported by 47 per cent of respondents and spaced repetition by 27 per cent (58 students).

| Technique reported | Students | Mean exam mark (users) | Mean exam mark (non-users) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spaced repetition | 58 | 72.9 | 67.0 |
| Practice testing | 100 | 71.0 | 66.5 |
| Re-reading notes | 172 | 68.4 | 69.7 |
| Highlighting | 136 | 68.1 | 69.6 |

Students who used spaced repetition or practice testing had higher mean marks than those who did not. Re-reading and highlighting showed small differences in the other direction, which are well within what could be expected by chance in a sample of this size. Students who used spaced repetition were also more likely to be in their second year and to have attended one of our study-skills workshops, so it is not possible to separate the technique from these other factors.

### 3.3 Paid work

Overall, 63 per cent of respondents had a paid job during the examination period. Students working more than 15 hours a week (46 students) had a mean mark of 64.2, compared with 69.9 for students working 15 hours or fewer, including those not working at all. These students also reported less sleep on average.

### 3.4 Study time

Reported hours of study outside classes had only a weak positive correlation with examination marks (r = 0.18). Several students with high reported study hours had below-average marks, and several with modest study hours had high marks. How students studied seemed to matter at least as much as how long.

### 3.5 Caffeine and wellbeing

Students who reported drinking three or more energy drinks a week reported lower wellbeing scores and less sleep. We found no clear relationship between caffeine use and examination marks once sleep was taken into account, but the sample is too small to say this with confidence.

## 4. Discussion

The patterns in our data are broadly similar to those described in the published literature: students who reported more sleep, and students who used active study techniques such as spaced repetition and practice testing, had higher examination marks on average. Heavy paid work was associated with lower marks.

However, our survey **cannot show cause and effect**. There are several reasons for caution:

1. **Self-selection.** Only about one in three invited students responded. Students who respond to a survey about study habits may be more organised, or more interested in their studies, than those who do not.
2. **Self-report.** Sleep, study hours and techniques were reported by students themselves, looking back over several weeks. People often overestimate their sleep and study time.
3. **Confounding.** The groups we compared differ in many ways at once. Students who sleep less also work more hours and drink more caffeine; students who use spaced repetition are more often in their second year and have more often attended a workshop. Any of these could explain part of the differences in marks.
4. **Small sample and uneven representation.** With 212 respondents, and with some departments over- or under-represented, the estimates are imprecise and may not apply to the college as a whole.
5. **Reverse direction.** It is possible that students who were already doing well felt less stressed and therefore slept better, rather than sleep improving their results.

For these reasons, we do not recommend presenting these figures to students as evidence that changing one habit will raise their marks by a particular number of points.

## 5. Recommendations

Despite these limits, the findings are consistent with existing evidence and support some practical steps:

1. **Continue and expand the study-skills workshops**, with more emphasis on practice testing and spaced repetition, which fewer than half of students currently use.
2. **Include sleep in wellbeing guidance during examination periods**, presented as general good practice rather than as a guaranteed way to raise marks.
3. **Work with the careers and finance offices** to help students who need to work long hours find more flexible jobs, or to identify hardship funding.
4. **Run a better-designed follow-up.** A future study should invite a random sample of students with follow-up reminders to raise the response rate, use sleep diaries rather than memory, and, if resources allow, test a workshop programme by comparing students who are offered it with students who are offered it later.

## 6. Ethics and Data Protection

The survey was approved by the college's research ethics committee. Participation was voluntary, and students could withdraw at any point before submitting. Linking to examination marks was done only for students who gave explicit consent. The research team never saw names or student numbers. Data will be deleted three years after publication of this report.
